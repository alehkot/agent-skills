# Clarify preferences with small alternatives

Use this optional technique when a user cannot easily describe a preference or
an ambiguous phrase such as "simple" supports different experiences. Two short
examples can expose the tradeoff more clearly than another abstract question.
Skip it when a direct question or reading an available fact resolves the issue.
It is part of the current interview, not a new stage or an implementation task.

## Choose the smallest useful contrast

Identify one unresolved choice whose prerequisites are already settled. Offer two
plausible versions that differ on that choice: short example outputs, a textual
flow, a sketch, or a counterexample. Keep unrelated details comparable and explain
the benefit and cost of each fairly. Do not make the preferred version polished
and the alternative deliberately unattractive. Include "neither" or an opportunity
to correct the premise; two examples are not an exhaustive option set.

Label examples and synthetic data as hypothetical. State material assumptions;
do not present a mockup as implemented behavior, a source fact, or tested evidence.
Use conversation text unless an artifact would materially help and its creation
is permitted. Do not install tools, contact services or publish a prototype solely
because examples would be useful.

For example, if "quick incident summary" is ambiguous, show the same invented
incident as (A) a two-sentence status/next-action summary and (B) a compact timeline
with the same facts. Ask which would help the intended reader act and why. Do not
change certainty or omit the unresolved cause to make one option look better.

## Ask for the criterion, not just the favorite

Attach a recommendation and rationale, clearly labeled as your proposal. Ask what
makes the selected option useful or what both miss. A reaction may reveal a
criterion, not agreement with every incidental detail of the example. Log the
confirmed choice and its reason separately from assumptions, rejected examples
and any still-open dependent decisions. Honor explicit delegation without asking
again for choices already covered by it.

Preferences do not settle facts. If the choice depends on an unknown legal rule,
platform capability, cost or observed behavior, investigate the appropriate
source first or mark that dependency unresolved. Do not invite the user to vote
on a factual claim or infer a permanent preference from a single example.

## Keep the existing limits

Use the existing one-question or independent-question round and remaining user
allowance. Preparing or showing examples does not reset an interview limit or
justify an extra question after it expires. If the user asks to stop, stop; if
no reaction is available, leave the choice open. A preference response is not
permission to implement, upload, publish or change unrelated decisions.

No fixed sequence of interview stages, mandatory mockup or separate workflow is
required by this technique.
